A person buried near Rome in 300 AD was likely to have ancestry pointing east, toward Greece and the Levant. A person buried in the same ground 500 years later was likely to have ancestry pointing north.

The genomic history of the Roman region contains two reversals, not one.

The first came as the empire expanded, when ancestry in and around Rome shifted strongly toward the eastern Mediterranean. The second came as it contracted: through late antiquity and into the medieval period, ancestry moves back toward **central and northern European** populations ([Antonio et al., *Science*, 2019](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aay6826), PMID: 31699931).

## What a "migration period" looks like in DNA

The historical narrative of this era is written around named peoples — Goths, Lombards, Vandals, Franks — arriving in a weakening empire.

Genetics complicates that framing in a useful way. What the data shows is a change in ancestry proportions over time in sampled populations. It does not show ethnic groups moving as intact units, and it does not validate the tidy arrows drawn on textbook maps.

Work on early medieval cemeteries elsewhere in Europe has found burial communities containing both incoming and local ancestry, in the same graveyards, sometimes in the same families. Whatever "the Lombards" were, they were not genetically homogeneous.

The population changed. The story of *which named people did it* is much harder to pin down, and archaeologists and geneticists are still arguing about it — appropriately.

## Why this era is genuinely difficult

**Named groups were political, not genetic.** Identity in late antiquity could be adopted, imposed, or claimed. A person buried with Gothic material culture need not have had Gothic ancestry, whatever that would mean.

**Sampling is thin and skewed.** Cemeteries that were excavated, published and successfully sequenced are not a random sample of anybody.

**The changes are proportional, not total.** Nothing here describes replacement of a population. It describes a shifting mixture.
