Among people under thirty, 24.2% describe themselves as morning people. Among people over sixty, 63.1% do.

That is the same species, largely the same gene pool, and a completely different answer. Whatever makes someone a lark, it is not fixed at conception.

![Watercolour of a kitchen at dawn, one person awake](/blog/images/morning-person/dawn-kitchen.png)
*Chronotype shifts across a lifetime. The genome does not.*

## The study

Researchers analysed self-reported morningness in **89,283 individuals** of European ancestry, testing around 8 million genotyped or imputed markers. They identified **15 genome-wide significant loci**, seven of them close to well-established circadian genes including *PER2* ([Hu et al., *Nature Communications*, 2016](https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms10448)).

Pathway analysis pointed at two systems: circadian regulation, and phototransduction — the machinery by which the retina converts light into the signal that sets the clock in the hypothalamus.

That second one is the more interesting finding. Part of what separates a lark from an owl may be how a person's eyes report the arrival of morning.

## What morningness travels with

In the same cohort, morning people were:

| | Morning people | Night people |
|---|---|---|
| Insomnia | 12.9% | 18.3% |
| Odds of needing >8h sleep | OR 0.67 | — |
| Odds of depression | OR 0.64 | — |

Morningness was also more common in women (48.4%) than men (39.7%).

Here is where the paper does something many popular write-ups skip. The authors tested whether these associations were *causal* using Mendelian randomisation — and **found no evidence that they were**. Morningness travels alongside lower depression and lower BMI in this data. It does not follow that becoming a morning person would change either.

## Age beats genotype

Return to the opening numbers. Chronotype moves enormously across a lifetime — from roughly a quarter of under-thirties to nearly two-thirds of over-sixties preferring mornings.

No genetic variant in this study comes close to that effect size. Each of the 15 loci nudges the odds. Age, light exposure, shift work, having a newborn, and the simple fact of getting older move the needle far more.

Anyone selling you a genetic explanation for why you cannot get up should be asked how it accounts for the fact that you probably will, eventually, without any change to your DNA.

[Sleep Zzzz](https://www.geneplaza.com/app-store/54) applies the variants identified by Hu and colleagues to your genotype and shows where you fall on the morningness distribution from that study.

Read it as a mild tendency, measured in a large but ancestrally narrow sample, against a trait that visibly changes with age. It is a genuine signal. It is not an excuse, and it is not a schedule.
